Reading the plates

One recurrence, two grids, and a catalogue of everything that stays bounded.

  1. Give every pixel a complex number

    Plate I maps the frame to c ∈ [−2.0, 0.7] × [−1.3, 1.3]. Plate II maps it to starting values z₀ and holds c still. Same code, different meaning of a pixel.

  2. Iterate in double precision

    z ← z² + c, repeated. For z² + c the escape test is exact: once |z| > 2, the orbit can never return, so any R ≥ 2 is a valid trap.

  3. Count the escape

    The raw count n is a staircase — visible banding. Subtract log(log|z| / log R) / log R and the staircase becomes a continuous height field; that is what the palettes ride on.

  4. Colour the survivors

    Points that never escape are the set. Rather than paint them black, both plates detect the attracting cycle the orbit falls into and tint it by period — the interior becomes a map of dynamics.

  5. Refine as you descend

    Iteration budget grows with depth (≈ +28 per binary zoom), so detail survives to the double-precision wall near 10¹⁴×, where pixels start stepping.

The Mandelbrot set is not a sibling of the Julia sets — it is their table of contents.

A parameter c lies inside ℳ exactly when 𝒥c is one connected piece. Step outside the boundary and 𝒥c shatters into a Cantor dust of uncountably many points. That is why hovering Plate I is a topology preview of Plate II: the rabbit, the airfoil, the seahorse valleys near c ≈ −0.75, and the tiny satellite copies along the real axis are all entries in the same index.
