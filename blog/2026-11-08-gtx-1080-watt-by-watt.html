Watt by watt: tuning a ten-year-old GTX 1080 for local LLMs

8 November 2026 · Peter + Hermes Agent · one afternoon of A/B testing on erwing
TL;DR — Our ten-year-old GTX 1080 box runs a 9B local LLM around the clock and was plateauing at 93–94 °C. A repaste recovered 13 °C. A thermal-cap A/B then proved the cap was never the constraint — the power limit is. A full 160/180/200 W power-limit curve showed prefill tracks the clocks while generation stays flat, and a memory-clock A/B ended in a dead end: the driver flat-out refuses to lock GDDR5X clocks on Pascal. The invalid test still paid — it measured a ±1.3% run-to-run noise floor, which reclassified our “160 W generation penalty” as noise. We are now running 160 W “for a while”: ~4–6 °C cooler, a much quieter fan, a real ~5% prefill cost, and generation that is statistically free.

Why tune a 2016 GPU

erwing, the lab’s ten-year-old box, carries a GTX 1080 8 GB (GDDR5X, 10 Gbps, 180 W stock) and has been serving Qwen3.5 9B (Q4_K_M) through llama-server on 24/7 duty. After months of that, the card was plateauing at 93–94 °C under a modest benchmark load — within three degrees of the silicon’s ~96–97 °C hardware-slowdown point, on paste that was older than most of the lab’s hardware.

The question wasn’t “is this card slow” — it is, and we accept that — but where exactly does it leak performance: thermals? the power cap? memory bandwidth? Every answer below came from an A/B run in the same thermal window, never from vibes.

Step 1 — the repaste (the one lever that mattered for temperature)

Fresh thermal paste (and pads) went on during the 7 November reboot window. The before/after is about as clean as a variable gets:

Everything downstream — every A/B, every power point — was measured on the repasted card.

Step 2 — the thermal-cap A/B: the cap was never the constraint

With 15 °C of headroom back, the obvious next move was to raise the software target-temperature cap (driver 581.57, nvidia-smi -gtt, range 60–92 °C) from the default 83 °C to 86 °C. The working hypothesis: if the cap were biting, prefill (compute- and clock-bound) should gain, while generation (bandwidth-bound) should not.

Both arms ran from the same cold state, with the cap as the only variable — a ~10K-token unique prefill plus a 600-token generation, measured at the plateau:

The cap was never the binding constraint after the repaste: the power limit was already pinning the clocks long before 83 °C would. We kept 86 °C as headroom for the power-limit tests below. (House footnote: the first attempt at this A/B was voided because the flag is -gtt, not -lt — the “86 °C” arm had silently run at 83. The redo, cold-to-cold, is what the table shows.)

Read together: prefill is clock-bound — it tracks the power limit with diminishing returns (160→180 buys +5.1%, 180→200 buys +3.8%) — while generation is flat across the whole range (29.7–30.3, which we will soon explain is entirely noise). We deliberately did not test 217 W (the driver’s max): it would have made 86 °C the binding cap again, which is exactly the thing we just got rid of.

The throttle bit that finally told the truth

Through the whole session, nvidia-smi’s throttle-reason field kept showing 0x…0001 — “HW thermal protection” — at 33 °C, idle, with the fan at 30%. Stale latching: the bits remember a past event and are not a live signal.

Then, at 160 W, during prefill, the field showed 0x…0004 (HW power brake) together with a visible clock dip to 1594 MHz — the first genuine throttle signal of the session. The pattern that matters: the clock dip is the signal, the bit is the latched echo. At 160 W the cap bites hardest exactly where the card is most compute-hungry; at 180/200 W the same prefill never hit a brake.

The memory-speed dead end

The flat generation column begged for proof: decode re-reads the whole model from VRAM every token, so if it is bandwidth-bound, cutting memory bandwidth ~10% should cut generation ~10%. The driver’s help text even lists -lmc (lock memory clocks), and the card reports four VRAM P-states — 5005 MHz (320 GB/s) under load, with 4513/810/405 listed as “supported”.

It did not work. The GPU refuses: 

nvidia-smi -lmc 4513
Setting locked Memory clocks is not supported for GPU 00000000:01:00.0.
All done.

Pascal’s GDDR5X is fused to a single speed under load; the other P-state entries are listings, not lockable values. The “4513 vs 5005” A/B we ran (both arms under the 160 W cap so core clocks were identical) was void the moment that line printed — arm B never left 5005 MHz, so the reported −0.33% delta was two identical conditions. We are discarding it, not publishing it as a finding. The mechanistic question “would faster memory help generation?” is simply unanswerable on this card: there is no lever to pull.

What the invalid test actually measured

The discarded A/B ran five 600-token generations in total — all of them at the same settings, which happens to be exactly what a noise-floor measurement requires:

±0.4 tok/s, i.e. ±1.3%, is just run-to-run variance on this box—engine—model combination. Rescoring the power curve against that band: the 160 W generation figure (29.7, nominally −1.3% vs 30.1) sits exactly at the edge of the noise, and the 200 W one (+0.7%) is inside it. So the honest statement is not “160 W costs 1.3% of generation” but “generation is statistically insensitive to the power limit across 160–200 W” — which is the bandwidth-bound model surviving on better evidence, since the core clocks moved ±70 MHz the whole time.

Where we landed: 160 W, for a while

The standing order is to run 160 W + 86 °C for a stretch of normal use before deciding permanently. The trade on the table:

  • 160 W — 79–80 °C plateau, fan ≤62%, 145–158 W draw, 6–7 °C of headroom to the cap. Real cost: ~5% of prefill. Generation: statistically free.
  • 180 W (stock) — the balanced point: 82 °C, 4 °C headroom, everything at its as-sold speed.
  • 200 W — maximum speed (+3.8% prefill) at 84–85 °C, 75% fan and 1–2 °C of headroom. We would only pick this for heavy batch work.

One ops footnote for the brave: -pl and -gtt are both volatile — a reboot silently reverts erwing to 180 W / 83 °C. Making any of the above permanent is a one-line fold into the box’s startup task; the decision waits for the observation window to finish.

Lessons

  • Repaste first, tune second. A 13 °C paste swap beat every software knob we then tried.
  • Find the binding constraint before A/B-ing anything else. The thermal cap was never binding after the repaste; the power limit was. The null 83-vs-86 test is what proved it.
  • On a fused-memory card, the power limit is the only real knob. Prefill tracks it; generation does not.
  • Throttle-reason bits latch. Trust the clock dip, not the bit — the “thermal protection” flag at 33 °C idle is a ghost, while the power-brake bit plus a 1594 MHz dip is a confession.
  • Measure your noise floor before believing single-digit-percent deltas. ±1.3% run-to-run is the size of the effects we were chasing.
  • A dead-end test can still pay. The invalid memory-clock A/B produced the noise floor that fixed the interpretation of the entire power curve.
